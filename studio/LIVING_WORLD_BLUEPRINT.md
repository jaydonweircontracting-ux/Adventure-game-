Yes. We need to think much bigger than just gathering wood, building houses and growing villages. If Adventure is going to become a genuinely living world, we need the systems that make a civilization function, the natural systems that sustain it, and the systems that allow it to change over generations.

I would expand the master design into a complete world, ecology, survival, settlement, economy, technology and civilization simulation.

The key is that all of these systems must affect one another—not operate as isolated menus or decorative features.

Adventure — Complete Living World Feature Blueprint

## 1. Natural world and geography

The world needs to feel geographically believable and have natural processes.

* Terrain: plains, hills, mountains, valleys, cliffs, plateaus, beaches and wetlands.
* Water: oceans, rivers, streams, lakes, ponds, waterfalls, springs and underground water.
* Geology: tectonic plates, rock formations, erosion, sediment, caves and underground layers.
* Soil: fertility, moisture, nutrients, contamination and different soil types.
* Climate: temperature, rainfall, wind, humidity, snow and regional weather.
* Biomes: forests, grasslands, tundra, deserts, swamps, jungles and alpine regions.
* Vegetation: trees, bushes, grass, flowers, mushrooms, crops and medicinal plants.
* Natural formations: boulders, cliffs, caves, ravines, beaches and mineral deposits.

Important additions:

* Rivers should flow according to terrain elevation.
* Water should influence settlement placement and agriculture.
* Forests should spread, mature, burn and regenerate.
* Soil should degrade or improve depending on how it is used.
* Mountains should contain different mineral deposits at different depths.
* Weather should affect travel, farming, construction and survival.

## 2. Natural resources and production chains

We need a complete resource economy.

| Category | Resources |
|---|---|
| Wood | Logs, branches, planks, hardwood |
| Stone | Limestone, granite, sandstone, slate |
| Minerals | Iron, copper, tin, gold, silver |
| Refined materials | Iron bars, steel, bronze, glass |
| Agriculture | Wheat, barley, oats, vegetables, fruit |
| Food | Meat, fish, bread, milk, cheese, eggs |
| Animals | Hides, leather, wool, bones, feathers |
| Natural | Clay, sand, salt, charcoal, herbs |
| Advanced | Tools, weapons, armour, machinery |

Every resource needs:

* A world location.
* A quantity and quality.
* A method of extraction.
* Appropriate tools and professions.
* Storage requirements.
* Transportation requirements.
* Production recipes.
* Consumption and trade value.
* Regeneration rules where applicable.

We should also introduce resource depletion, scarcity, renewable resources and finite deposits.

## 3. Complete building and infrastructure catalogue

We should research the building progression of Cossacks 3 and Age of Empires as reference points, then develop our own building system.

| Building group | Examples |
|---|---|
| Residential | Tents, cottages, houses, apartments, manor houses |
| Agriculture | Farms, barns, granaries, mills, pastures |
| Forestry | Lumber camps, sawmills, wood storage |
| Mining | Mines, quarries, smelters, ore storage |
| Manufacturing | Blacksmiths, workshops, tanneries, carpenters |
| Food production | Bakeries, butcher shops, fisheries, breweries |
| Trade | Markets, trading posts, warehouses, caravanserais |
| Civic | Town halls, councils, courthouses, town squares |
| Religion and culture | Temples, shrines, libraries, schools |
| Military | Barracks, archery ranges, stables, armouries |
| Defence | Walls, gates, watchtowers, castles |
| Infrastructure | Roads, bridges, wells, aqueducts, docks |
| Advanced | Universities, engineering workshops, foundries |

Every building should have:

* A footprint and construction stages.
* Required materials and labour.
* Workers and production capacity.
* Storage and inventory.
* Maintenance and operating costs.
* Environmental requirements.
* Upgrades and technology requirements.
* Damage, deterioration and repair.
* A functional purpose within the settlement.

Buildings should be usable by the player and autonomously constructed by NPCs.

## 4. NPCs need to become individual simulated people

This is one of the biggest parts of the project.

### Individual NPC systems

* Identity, name, age and appearance.
* Personality, traits and temperament.
* Skills, experience and intelligence.
* Needs, desires and personal goals.
* Inventory, equipment and clothing.
* Health, injuries and illnesses.
* Memory and personal history.
* Family, friendships and relationships.
* Morale, happiness and stress.
* Profession, workplace and schedule.
* Housing and personal belongings.

### NPC daily activities

* Wake up and sleep.
* Eat and drink.
* Travel to work.
* Gather resources.
* Operate workshops.
* Haul materials.
* Construct buildings.
* Visit markets.
* Socialize and form relationships.
* Raise children.
* Defend their settlement.
* Explore and travel.
* Respond to emergencies.

### Population lifecycle

* Birth and childhood.
* Education and apprenticeships.
* Adult professions.
* Marriage and family.
* Ageing.
* Illness and injury.
* Death and inheritance.
* Population migration.

The population should change naturally rather than simply increasing through a counter.

## 5. Animal and wildlife ecosystem

Animals should have their own AI and ecosystem.

### Wildlife

* Deer, elk, rabbits, wolves, bears, foxes and boars.
* Birds, fish, reptiles and insects.
* Predators and prey.
* Migration, reproduction and territorial behaviour.

### Livestock

* Cows, sheep, goats, pigs, chickens and horses.
* Breeding and domestication.
* Feeding and grazing.
* Animal products.
* Herd management.
* Stables and animal shelters.

### Ecosystem interactions

* Predators hunt prey.
* Animals consume vegetation.
* Overhunting reduces populations.
* Forest loss changes animal habitats.
* Disease spreads between populations.
* Wildlife migrates as seasons change.

NPCs should be able to hunt, fish, farm, raise livestock and manage herds.

## 6. Food, farming and survival

We need an actual food economy.

* Crop planting, growth, harvesting and storage.
* Different growing seasons.
* Soil fertility and irrigation.
* Crop rotation and fertilization.
* Livestock feeding and breeding.
* Hunting and fishing.
* Food preparation and cooking.
* Food quality and nutrition.
* Spoilage, refrigeration and preservation.
* Food shortages and famine.
* Water wells and drinking water.
* Disease and sanitation.

A town with no food should experience consequences. A settlement with productive farmland should be able to support a larger population.

## 7. Logistics and transportation

This is critical. A civilization cannot function if resources magically teleport between buildings.

* Individual carrying capacity.
* Carts, wagons and pack animals.
* Roads and road quality.
* Bridges and river crossings.
* River boats and ships.
* Warehouses and stockpiles.
* Delivery priorities.
* Supply and demand.
* Travel distance and travel time.
* Trade routes and caravan schedules.
* Transport costs and risks.
* Road maintenance.
* Blocked routes and detours.

NPCs need to find resources, reserve them, collect them and deliver them to the correct destination.

## 8. Economy, markets and trade

The settlement should have a real economic life.

* Resource prices and market demand.
* Supply shortages and surpluses.
* Production costs.
* Wages and salaries.
* Personal wealth.
* Taxes and settlement income.
* Currency and banking.
* Merchants and travelling traders.
* Trade agreements.
* Imports and exports.
* Market competition.
* Resource specialization.
* Economic prosperity and collapse.

Different settlements should develop different economic strengths depending on their geography.

A mountain settlement might become a mining centre, while a riverside settlement becomes a fishing and trade centre.

## 9. Technology and civilization progression

We need an actual development system rather than simply unlocking buildings from a menu.

Possible progression:

* Primitive survival.
* Early villages.
* Agricultural development.
* Basic metallurgy.
* Organized settlements.
* Advanced construction.
* Commercial development.
* Military specialization.
* Engineering and scientific advancement.
* Advanced civilizations.

Research should require resources, knowledge, specialists, infrastructure and time.

Technologies can unlock:

* New buildings.
* Better tools.
* Improved agriculture.
* More efficient production.
* Stronger weapons and armour.
* Transportation improvements.
* Better construction techniques.
* New governance systems.

## 10. Kingdoms, politics and diplomacy

Settlements should eventually become part of larger civilizations.

* Settlement leadership.
* Mayors, chiefs, kings and councils.
* Kingdom borders.
* Factions and alliances.
* Laws and criminal justice.
* Taxes and public services.
* Diplomacy and treaties.
* Immigration and citizenship.
* Territorial disputes.
* Rebellions and civil wars.
* Espionage and intelligence.
* Cultural identity.
* Succession and inheritance.

Kingdoms should have independent goals, interests and relationships.

## 11. Military, warfare and defence

* Recruitment and military professions.
* Training and experience.
* Infantry, archers, cavalry and siege units.
* Equipment and supply requirements.
* Commanders and leadership.
* Patrols and guard schedules.
* Fortifications and defensive planning.
* Sieges and assaults.
* Raids and ambushes.
* Military logistics.
* Wounds, casualties and morale.
* Territory occupation.
* Postwar rebuilding.

Armies need food, weapons, armour, wages and transportation. They should not be free units that appear from nowhere.

## 12. World events and natural disasters

The world needs unpredictable events.

* Forest fires.
* Flooding.
* Drought.
* Severe winters.
* Storms.
* Earthquakes.
* Landslides.
* Disease outbreaks.
* Crop failures.
* Animal migrations.
* Resource discoveries.
* Bandit attacks.
* Refugees and migration.
* Trade disruptions.
* Political uprisings.
* Wars and invasions.

Events should emerge from the simulation where possible, not just randomly trigger scripted notifications.

## 13. Exploration, adventure and player interaction

We must preserve what makes Adventure an adventure RPG.

* Dungeons and caves.
* Ancient ruins.
* Abandoned settlements.
* Hidden treasure.
* Rare resources.
* Quest systems.
* Hunting and gathering.
* Crafting and equipment.
* NPC conversations.
* Reputation and faction relationships.
* Player-owned property.
* Player businesses.
* Mercenary contracts.
* Exploration and discovery.
* Settlement assistance and management.

The player should be able to become an adventurer, merchant, builder, landowner, soldier, ruler or something in between.

We also need a powerful World Studio.

* Terrain editing.
* Water and river editing.
* Biome painting.
* Tree and vegetation placement.
* Mineral deposit placement.
* Building placement and templates.
* Road and bridge construction.
* NPC and animal placement.
* Settlement creation.
* Faction and kingdom creation.

* Simulation speed controls.

## 15. The systems that tie absolutely everything together

This is arguably the most important section.

We need shared underlying systems that every feature uses.

| Foundation | Responsibility |
|---|---|
| World database | Authoritative terrain, entities and world objects |
| Entity/component system | Common representation of people, animals and objects |
| Event system | Communication between simulation systems |
| Resource registry | One authoritative resource and inventory model |
| Job scheduler | Work discovery, reservation and assignment |
| Navigation | Pathfinding, terrain costs and obstacle avoidance |
| Logistics | Carrying, storage, delivery and transport |
| Production | Recipes, inputs, outputs and work rates |
| Construction | Building plans, labour, materials and completion |
| Needs AI | Individual NPC survival and goals |
| Society simulation | Population, families, relationships and culture |
| Economy | Markets, prices, wages and settlement finances |
| Civilization AI | Expansion, diplomacy, research and warfare |
| Simulation clock | Time, seasons, schedules and progression |
| Persistence | Save/load, versioning and world continuity |
| Performance manager | Chunk simulation, background updates and LOD |

One especially important addition: simulation levels of detail.

We cannot simulate every NPC, animal, tree and building at full detail all the time.

* Nearby: full AI, movement, animation and interactions.
* Nearby settlements: detailed economic and job simulation.
* Distant settlements: aggregated population, production and consumption.
* Remote regions: simplified ecological and civilization updates.

This will let Adventure grow into a huge world without destroying performance.

## 16. The actual development priorities

We should build all of this, but not attempt to implement everything simultaneously.

Adventure development sequence

* Unified simulation foundation
  Shared world state, entity identity, events, clock, persistence and system integration.
  First priority
* Living natural environment
  Terrain, water, soil, vegetation, minerals, weather, seasons and wildlife.
* Resource and logistics economy
  Harvesting, mining, inventories, stockpiles, hauling, crafting and production.
* Construction and infrastructure
  Complete building catalogue, construction jobs, roads, bridges, utilities and maintenance.
* Autonomous population
  NPC needs, jobs, families, relationships, schedules, memory and professions.
* Self-sustaining settlements
  Food, housing, trade, markets, population growth and autonomous expansion.
* Civilization simulation
  Technology, government, culture, kingdoms, diplomacy, warfare and territorial growth.
* World Studio and large-scale optimization
  Full editing tools, simulation controls, diagnostics, save/load and scalable world simulation.

## 17. The ultimate gameplay goal

Imagine starting a new world.

You begin with wilderness.

* Animals inhabit the forests.
* Rivers carve through the landscape.
* Resources are distributed throughout the world.
* A few NPCs establish a settlement.
* They gather wood and stone.
* They build shelter.
* They establish farms and livestock.
* They create workshops and markets.
* Their population grows.
* Other people arrive.
* Roads connect neighbouring settlements.
* Merchants establish trade routes.
* Technology advances.
* Towns become cities.
* Cities form kingdoms.
* Kingdoms compete, cooperate and fight.
* The landscape changes as civilizations expand.

And all of this continues while you are out exploring dungeons, fighting monsters, completing quests or building your own home.

The world should not need the player to exist. The player should become part of a world that already exists.

That's the direction I'd establish for Adventure: a persistent, evolving, autonomous world simulation with an adventure RPG built into it.

One final architectural rule: we should maintain a feature dependency map and an automated integration test for every major gameplay loop. That way, adding a new feature cannot silently break the systems it depends on.
